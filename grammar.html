1. Alphabetical Order & Dictionary Skills

Quick Revision

Alphabetical order
Arranging words in the order in which their letters appear in the alphabet (A to Z). If two words start with the same letter, compare their second letter; if that also matches, compare the third letter, and so on.
Example: ball, belt, bin, bun (compare 2nd letter)  |  jacket, jam, jar, jaw (compare 3rd letter)
Headword
The word being defined in a dictionary entry, usually printed in bold.
Example: poke, poker, polar, pole, police
Pronunciation
How a word is said aloud, shown between two slash marks in a dictionary.
Example: police /pa'li:s/
Part of speech
A label showing what type of word it is. Common abbreviations: Adj = adjective, Adv = adverb, n or N = noun, v or vb = verb, prep = preposition.
Guide words
The two words printed at the top of a dictionary page, showing the first and last word defined on that page.
Example: Guide words 'dragon' and 'duct' tell you the word 'drive' will be found on that page.

2. The Sentence

Quick Revision

Sentence
An arrangement of a group of words that makes complete sense.
Rule
A sentence begins with a capital letter and usually ends with a full stop. A sentence always has a verb.
Statement
A sentence which says or states something. It ends with a full stop (.).
Example: My favourite colour is red.
Question
A sentence which asks something. It ends with a question mark (?).
Example: What is your favourite colour?
Yes/No questions
Simple questions answered with Yes or No are made by changing the word order of a statement. Use 'is/are' with the noun's number: one thing/person → is, more than one → are. Use are/do with 'you'.
Example: This is my bag. → Is this my bag?  |  These apples are fresh. → Are these apples fresh?

3. Subject and Predicate

Quick Revision

Sentence parts
Every sentence tells us about something or someone, and what they do or are. A sentence has two parts: the subject and the predicate.
Subject
The main person, object or place that the sentence is about. Ask 'Who or what is the sentence about?' to find it. The subject can be one word or several words.
Example: The dog is barking. → ‘The dog’ is the subject.
Predicate
The part of the sentence that tells us more about the subject — what the subject is doing or being. Ask 'What is happening?' to find it. A predicate always has a verb (the verb is always part of the predicate).
Example: The dog is barking. → ‘is barking’ is the predicate.

4. Negative Sentences

Quick Revision

Positive sentence
A statement that tells you something is so.
Negative sentence
A statement that uses the word no or not.
How to make a sentence negative
Place 'not' after is/am/are  |  place 'not' after will/shall/can (can + not = cannot)  |  add do/does before 'not' for other verbs.
Example: They are not my new neighbours.  |  I cannot ride a bicycle.  |  We do not have pets.  |  Uma does not go to playschool.
Keep in mind
'Do' is used with I, we, you, they and naming words that are more than one in number. 'Does' is used with he, she, it, and a naming word that is one in number.

5. Nouns: Common, Proper and Collective

Quick Revision

Noun
The name of a person, place, animal or thing. Formula to remember: Noun = Name of Anything.
Common noun
Names any person, place, thing or animal in general (a class or type). Does not begin with a capital letter.
Example: boy, market, book, lion, school
Proper noun
Names one particular person, place, thing or animal. Always begins with a capital letter.
Example: Ravi, New Market, the Shatabdi Express, Simba
Collective noun
Names a collection or group of people, animals, birds or things. Though it refers to many, it is treated as one single thing.
Example: a class of students, a pack of dogs, a flock of birds, a team of players, a bunch of grapes
Abstract noun
Names a feeling or idea that cannot be touched, but can be felt or thought. Tip: ask ‘Can I touch it?’ — if no, it is probably abstract.
Example: love, joy, honesty, wisdom, happiness
Material noun
Names a material or substance used to make things (raw material).
Example: gold, wood, water, cotton, milk
Easy tricks
Trick 1: If you can see, touch, name or feel something, it's a noun. Trick 2: Try the sentence ‘I see a ___.’ If the word fits, it might be a noun.
Collective nouns to know
an army of soldiers · a choir of singers · a band of musicians · a troupe of dancers · a crowd of onlookers · a team of players · a colony of ants · a pack of wolves · a swarm of bees/flies · a shoal of fish · a litter of puppies · a herd of cows/cattle/deer · a bunch of keys/grapes · a flight of stairs · a galaxy of stars · a sheaf of papers · a fleet of ships · a string of pearls · a gaggle of geese · a gang of robbers · a brood of hens · a bundle of sticks · a pod of dolphins · a pride of lions · an uncommon one: a murder of crows, a parliament of owls

6. Nouns: Singular and Plural

Quick Revision

Singular / Plural
A noun is singular when it is one in number, and plural when it is more than one.
Example: one caterpillar → many caterpillars
Add -s
Most singular nouns form the plural by adding -s.
Example: book → books
Add -es
Nouns ending in -ss, -sh, -ch and -x add -es.
Example: class → classes, bush → bushes, watch → watches, tax → taxes
-y → -ies
Drop the -y and add -ies (when a consonant comes before the -y).
Example: study → studies, duty → duties
-y with a vowel before it
If a vowel comes right before the -y, just add -s.
Example: ray → rays, joy → joys, chimney → chimneys
-f / -fe → -ves
Most nouns ending in -f or -fe change to -ves.
Example: wolf → wolves, knife → knives, half → halves, loaf → loaves
Some -f nouns just add -s
A few nouns ending in -f simply add -s.
Example: roof → roofs, proof → proofs
-o → -oes or -os
Some nouns ending in -o add -es; others just add -s.
Example: potato → potatoes, buffalo → buffaloes  |  piano → pianos
Irregular plurals
Some nouns change completely in the plural.
Example: louse → lice, foot → feet, child → children, ox → oxen, woman → women, tooth → teeth, mouse → mice, goose → geese
No change
Some nouns are the same in singular and plural.
Example: sheep, fish, deer, aircraft (note: 'fishes' is used only for different kinds of fish)
Plural only
Some nouns are always used in the plural.
Example: trousers, oats, scissors, tongs, binoculars, spectacles
This/That
The plural of 'this' is 'these' and of 'that' is 'those'.

9. Articles: A, An, The

Quick Revision

A
Used before singular, countable nouns that begin with a consonant SOUND.
Example: a boy, a unicorn (starts with a 'y' sound), a European tourist (starts with a 'y' sound)
An
Used before singular, countable nouns that begin with a vowel SOUND (a, e, i, o, u).
Example: an elephant, an hour (the 'h' is silent), an honest man
The
Used before a specific noun — something known, unique, or already mentioned.
Example: the sun, the school, the Earth, the Himalayas, the Ganga
Memory chant
“A before b, c, d… An for a, e, i, o, u — you'll see! The is for the only one — like the Earth, the moon, the sun!”

fox (male) → vixen (female) · ram → ewe · peacock → peahen · buck → doe · rooster → hen · stallion → mare
Possessive ’s
To show that something belongs to someone, add ’s to a singular noun, and just an apostrophe (’) to a plural noun already ending in -s.
Example: Anil's bat · the dog's tail · the birds' nests · the men's cars
